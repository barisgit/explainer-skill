# What made pages better or worse

Each point comes from comparing generated pages side by side. Use judgment where your subject differs.

## The reader

- **Decision first:** write it like a spec, not a landing page: the claim, one sentence of scope, a figure made for that claim, a short interpretation. Start from the decision the reader faces and the one relation that settles it (for "how much", the step from one size to the next, not the totals), and cut what does not change that decision: illustrations, circuits, protocol detail and sections that do not bear on the choice. On a page for understanding or acting, apply the same cut to the reader's goal: orientation, a derivation or a worked example stay when they improve understanding.
- **Questions first:** organise around the reader's questions: pages following the data's hierarchy (phases, people, items) buried the first answer five clicks deep. Serving a decider and the people doing the work, start with the decider's questions and give the others their own sections. Aim for about one screen per reader question, the rest in a folded reference band. Give a lost or time-pressed reader one task at a time. A section's heading states its answer and its body shows it; folds and views hold supporting detail, not the finding. Give answer headings short `data-nav` labels for the rail, which a page with views has and a long page asks for with `contents: true`.
- **First screen:** a headline that states the finding with its number, which is the page's accent, one sentence of scope, and the whole first figure. A restating lede, a "Decide:" line, a meta line and a caption each add a type level to say what the screen already shows; what is obvious can be omitted and what can be shown need not be said. Three levels (display, text, label) are enough, and all text inside a figure or control is label. When the reader has a task to begin, its section comes first: with a deadline, the first step of the first task, any warm-up beside it. Say what was priced or measured: a plan opening on price without it failed when presented. The first figure carries the deciding relation, not a flowchart restating the prose. Keep later controls with their questions: pages putting every filter, summary number and caveat up front read as a wall.

## Many records

- **Story in front, records behind:** keep many records (tasks, costs, findings, requirements) in one data collection and compute every number in the story from it. Story sections give the essence, each claim linking to the branch of the complete list it rests on (illustrative: "Spike boards: 12 tasks, 70 h →"). The list stays on the main page, every item in the source's own order (its numbering, sequence or chronology), grouped with subtotals; views add depth (a static `View` per record for a few, one filled from `records` for many or heavy ones). Sort by value only where the ranking is the answer, and call it a ranking. A view tells one thing's own story, not the page's table filtered to rows that are mostly zero.
- **Hierarchy as disclosure:** a collection goes in `Records`, grouped by its own fields, each row a summary line with its numbers. A filter highlights matches in the tree rather than flattening it. Each item that opens says what it is, why it matters and, where the source says, what done means; each group gets a sentence on what it delivers. A row carries only what readers compare across rows; the rest goes, unrepeated, into the opened item. A marker (a `.chip` beside its word) encodes a category readers scan across rows, never decoration.

## Judging every item

- **Each item whole, where it is judged:** when the reader must approve, decide or answer for every item (a decisions review, an approval list), show each one complete with its question beside it: the situation (what the reader does and what happens), what happens now, the alternative (and what comparable tools do, when known) and a recommendation. A fifty-decision review built as collapsed groups with a drill-down per item forced its owner into every item for context. For single-choice reviews, `Records` with `open`, `reply` and `width: "full"` lets items be read and answered in their rows; an item with its own options or recommendation takes a `Question`, kept with the item, not behind a click or collapsed group.
- **Only what needs the reader:** list items needing a call; fold the rest. The reply bar shows answered/total; keep any headline count consistent with it. Label options A/B/C beside their evidence; when the options are the rows of a comparison, ask on the table itself (`Question` with `rows` and `cols`), and when a heading already asks the question, leave out `legend` rather than say it twice. Mark the recommendation with `recommended` rather than in the label, and give a `note` where the reader may want to say why. Include "both" or "test first" when they are real outcomes, and "need more context" so a gap in the page becomes an answer, not a guess. Forced single choices can misrepresent decisions: use `Question multiple` when several may be chosen (`Records.reply` is single-choice). Saved answers live in browser storage, which may not last for a `file://` page; reply's "Copy reply", or Send on a page `serve` serves, keeps important ones outside it.
- **Ask when the source cannot say:** when the source gives an item less than the reader needs to judge it, ask whoever asked for the page before building. A value the source does not give stays empty, never inferred from its group's heading.

## Explaining

- **Interaction that reveals:** a control answers a question the reader has, or captures a decision, and changes something visible at once; one per figure is usually enough. Readers valued hiding a dominant part to see the rest, presets that honestly end in "nothing fits", and clicking a mark or row to open its view. Start a model at its smallest instructive size. Label what a control changes, and offer a way back to a meaningful default. Compare states with aligned small multiples, not a switched-away view. Headlines, counts, labels and explanations that depend on a control follow it or name the default state. What no control changes (table rows, item lists, a view's body) stays outside client components, so it shows before scripts run, prints, and keeps the layout still. Ask reader-only decisions on the page as reply questions.
- **Markdown first:** prose, lists, tables and formulas stay Markdown, also between a client component's tags (its `{children}`). Inside a JSX expression such as `items.map(...)`, backticks are plain text and a `{x}` between them is evaluated. For a computed link destination, use `<a href={...}>`, not braces in a Markdown destination. `kit types > tsconfig.json` prepares the config; `bun --check` checks the imported TS/TSX and reports success or errors (not MDX expressions).
- **Stepping:** step through something only when its order is the point, and offer several playable cases, each step showing where we are, what just changed and what has accumulated; one narrow walkthrough lost to broader pages every time. Structure that does not change, such as a total built from parts or a system and its connections, is one labelled drawing with values on the marks (a flowchart whose stages carry their size or time in the label or on the edge; when the text and a table refer to its parts, number them with tags, `kit mermaid`): Back, Play and Next around it cost a row and reveal nothing, and an animated sequence diagram nobody needed is the same mistake.
- **Study material:** a page that added every widget to a course's slides lost the point: start from the source's figures, one interaction per idea usually being enough, when interaction helps. Worked examples come before practice: "try first" suits revision, not missing basics. Keep a formula sheet as reference (`kit math.css`). Practice uses plausible choices (`Question` with `answer`) or a written answer (`Question` without options, the model answer inside), hiding the explanation and figures that give it away; its heading asks the question, since one stating the answer gives it away.
- **Homework:** the task section walks the deliverables in the source's order and numbering, each hard one worked at its own scale, or as a rule with a way to check the result, with a code skeleton where the deliverable is code. What a step needs stays in the step, not in a fold. Flag where the source is wrong beside the step it affects, recomputed and quoting what the source prints: a rebuild reported a table error that did not exist and missed a real one. Ground the steps in the reader's own tools and starting point, and ask which toolchain they have; when a tool was not supplied, ask for it or have the reader run it bare for its usage.

## Words and trust

- **Words:** cut repetition that adds no understanding, not useful summaries, labels or worked examples. Skip kickers, subtitles and captions restating headings, hints rewording labels, advice on reading a number, and page-making notes (where it saves, review rounds, who checked what, earlier drafts, source keys, tracker/date lines, reply-button instructions, "how this was made" sections): readers took them as filler. No summary readout repeats the headline number (labels on its own marks are not repeats); keep cues that help the reader act. Write about the subject, not the conversation that produced the page: supplied figures are inputs, with a control when the result depends on them; earlier work appears as data, named with its sources.
- **Basis and honesty:** put sources and qualifications that affect how a claim reads beside it, especially for actionable values, and each candidate's datasheet or shop links in one consistent place on it, not in prose. What a number rests on is content, not filler: shared method and assumptions go once in a closing basis paragraph, detail in reference. State an item's uncertainty once, as a status, not in every cell. Unknown is not no; a security claim stays exactly as strong as its source, and the page's own reasoning is marked beside its claim. Omit assumptions that would not change the reader's action.

## Showing

- **Show the subject:** where its structure or appearance bears on the reader's decision, show it: a block diagram, the system in its context, the flow of the process being changed, or a photo, render or mockup when one exists. Charts of its numbers are not a picture of it. Import real photos, drawings or datasheet crops of the thing being judged, readable in size and captioned with what each proves; catalogue thumbnails that prove nothing do not count. Link a still for media that cannot work offline; no `file://` links, which point into one person's machine. Show a process whose order matters whole, its labels a few words. A circuit is a ```` ```schematic ```` netlist, so the wiring you state is the wiring drawn (`kit schematic`): label parts by name, link them to the prose with `data-ref`, and draw only what the section explains, a stage or loop rather than the whole board. Hand-build inline SVG for a picture Mermaid and a schematic cannot draw, or parts that move or respond beyond highlighting and the states a step or choice sets by ref: a `viewBox` about 360 wide with 14-unit text reads on a phone; strokes and text in `currentColor`, other colours from the kit variables, `var(--accent)` for the part in question; each part a `<g data-ref>` that its name in the prose shares. An SVG file in `<img>` follows the theme but cannot link its parts.
- **Charts:** choose the form from the claim: sections making different claims should not share a shape (bars on one scale for a gap, an area for energy moved, a `Chart` line over stacked columns for a surplus that disappears). Put names and values on the marks, and keep a legend, axis title or gridline only when it tells the reader what the marks do not. Use one accent with one meaning across the page, the finding: series default to muted inks told apart by lightness, a line also by its dash, so put the accent on the series or bar the page is about, and mark other distinctions by the accent's hollow or hatch state, named once on a mark; keep each compared thing in the same slot across charts and views. Say what a region or point of a figure means on it, in words (a `Plot` note: "surplus goes to the grid", or an arrow for a change the reader should follow), rather than in a legend or caption. Give exact values without hover, on the marks, through a readout or disclosed table; when the reader picks one of a plot's columns (a month of twelve), make the choice the axis (`axis: true` in `Explore`), so the picked column's key gives its values where a row of stats would repeat them beside the figure. A single value is a number (`.stats`), not a one-bar chart. Zoom, legend toggles and click-to-drill only when they help answer the question. Motion only where it shows the phenomenon: a figure changes from its current state after interaction, and nothing animates or replays on arrival.
- **Colour:** the accent is yours to choose, from the subject or the user's or project's brand, in one `:root` rule of `light-dark()` pairs: `--accent: light-dark(#1f5fbf, #7fb0ff)`, dark enough for text on the light paper and light enough on the dark, with `--accent-ink` beside it when white text does not read on the light one. Where the categories are colours by nature (energy sources, teams, parties, lines on a map), give them real hues the same way in `--c1`..`--c6`; other series keep the muted inks. `check` warns about a token too faint for its use.
- **Layout:** give a section the weight of its question rather than the shape of the one before; sections sharing one shape read as a template. Prose linked to a figure, diagram or code shares a screen with it in `.beside`, which keeps the figure in view while the text scrolls: readers of stacked pages zoomed out until nothing was readable. Figures sit plain on the page, their controls right above what they act on. On phones, stack and reflow: nothing scrolls sideways but data tables, wide formulas and diagrams too wide to shrink to readable text.
- **Language:** write in the language the reader uses with you, asking when the source's could be meant, and keep the source's terms where the reader must find them in it. A page not in English sets `lang` in its frontmatter, and `labels` translated from `kit labels`; English controls on a Slovenian page read as unfinished.
