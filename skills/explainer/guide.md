# What made pages better or worse

Each point comes from comparing generated pages side by side. Use judgment where your subject differs.

## The reader

- **Questions first:** organise around the reader's questions: pages following the data's hierarchy (phases, people, items) buried the first answer five clicks deep. Serving a decider and the people doing the work, start with the decider's questions and give the others their own sections. Aim for about one screen per reader question, the rest in a folded reference band: completeness is not the main path. Give a lost or time-pressed reader one task at a time with a small progress map: a lost reader abandoned the NDV homework page for its sheer size. A section's heading states its answer and its body shows it; folds and views hold supporting detail, not the finding. Answer headings were too long to scan in the rail: give them short `data-nav` labels, which on four or more sections give a long page its rail, views or not.
- **First screen:** use one short headline, not several decisions joined together; the lede says what it rests on, not a list of negatives. Write the next action and its size beneath the answer as a masthead paragraph opening with a bold label (`**Start:** change one bit, 2 minutes`), its section first and reference after; one decisions form began nine screens down. Orient newcomers: what this is, where it stands, what is wanted, and what is in and out of scope; a plan opening on price without saying what was priced failed when presented. The first figure carries the deciding relation, not a flowchart restating the prose. Keep later controls with their questions: pages putting every filter, summary number and caveat up front read as a wall.

## Many records

- **Story in front, records behind:** keep many records (tasks, costs, findings, requirements) in one data collection and compute every number in the story from it. Story sections give the essence, each claim linking to the branch of the complete list it rests on (illustrative: "Spike boards: 12 tasks, 70 h →"). The list stays on the main page, every item in the source's own order (its numbering, sequence or chronology), grouped with subtotals; views add depth: a static `View` per record for a few, one filled from `records` for a dozen or more, or heavy ones. Without the list, readers hopped between views to see the plan, and a plan sorted by hours lost the lead, who knew its tasks by their work-list numbers. Sort by value only where the ranking is the answer, and call it a ranking. A view tells one thing's own story, not the page's table filtered to rows that are mostly zero.
- **Hierarchy as disclosure:** a collection goes in `Records`, grouped by its own fields, each row a summary line with its numbers; its table and board show the same records sorted or by status. A filter highlights matches in the tree rather than flattening it. Each item that opens says what it is, why it matters and, where the source says, what done means; each group gets a sentence on what it delivers. A row carries only what readers compare across rows; the rest goes into the opened item, without repeating the row. A marker (a `.chip` beside its word) encodes a category readers scan across rows, never decoration. A ninety-item plan shown as a flat table of hours and prices, never saying what each item was, left its lead unable to defend the price.

## Judging every item

- **Each item whole, where it is judged:** when the reader must approve, decide or answer for every item (a decisions review, an approval list), show each one complete with its question beside it: the situation (what the reader does and what happens), what happens now, the alternative (and what comparable tools do, when known) and a recommendation. A review of fifty decisions built as a grouped list with a drill-down per item, groups collapsed, sent its owner, back after two days, into every item for context and gave no way to answer in the list. For single-choice reviews, use `Records` with `open`, `reply` and `width: "full"`, so items are read and answered in their rows (its options are one list, so items with their own recommendation each take a `Question`); otherwise keep each complete item and its `Question` together, not behind a click or collapsed group.
- **Only what needs the reader:** list items needing a call; fold the rest. The reply bar shows answered/total; keep any headline count consistent with it. Label options A/B/C beside their evidence, mark the recommendation with `recommended` rather than in the label, and give a `note` where the reader may want to say why. Include "both" or "test first" when they are real outcomes, and "need more context" so a gap in the page becomes an answer, not a guess. Forced single choices can misrepresent decisions: use `Question multiple` when several may be chosen (`Records.reply` is single-choice). Define unfamiliar terms where first used, or in a short list before the items sharing them. Saved answers live in browser storage, which may not last for a `file://` page; reply's "Copy reply" keeps important ones outside it.
- **Ask when the source cannot say:** when the source gives an item less than the reader needs to judge it, ask whoever asked for the page before building. A value the source does not give stays empty, never inferred from its group's heading: a page that marked every item in a group "held" for that reason was wrong for each of them.

## Explaining

- **Interaction that reveals:** a control answers a reader's question and changes something visible at once. Readers valued hiding a dominant part to see the rest, playing several scenarios, presets that honestly end in "nothing fits", and clicking a mark or row to open its view. Start a model at its smallest instructive size. Beside the first control, say what to try and what to notice (illustrative: "Change the input; notice which branch lights up"), and offer a way back to a meaningful default. Compare states with aligned small multiples rather than making readers remember a switched-away view. Headlines, counts, labels and explanations that depend on a control follow it or name the default state. What no control changes (table rows, item lists, a view's body) stays outside client components, so it shows before scripts run, prints, and keeps the layout still. Ask reader-only decisions on the page as reply questions, not in chat.
- **Markdown first:** prose, lists, tables and formulas stay Markdown, also between a client component's tags (its `{children}`). In JSX inside an expression such as `items.map(...)`, backticks are plain text and a `{x}` between them is evaluated. Your own component updates a control's state with a functional `setState((s) => ...)`, or values restored on reload are lost. Typecheck with `kit types > tsconfig.json`, then `bun --check`.
- **Stepping:** step through something only when its order is the point, and offer several playable cases, each step showing where we are, what just changed and what has accumulated; one narrow walkthrough lost to broader pages every time. A total built from parts, such as a cost waterfall, reads at a glance: show it finished with the values on the marks, since Back, Play and Next around it cost a row and reveal nothing.
- **Study material:** a page that added every widget to a course's slides lost the point: start from the source's figures, one interaction per idea usually being enough. Unfamiliar material gets a worked example before practice: "try first" suits revision, not missing basics. Keep a formula sheet as reference (`kit math.css`). Practice uses plausible choices (`Question` with `answer`) or a written answer (`Question` without options, the model answer inside, outside any reply), hiding the explanation and figures that give it away; its heading asks the question, since one stating the answer gives it away.
- **Homework:** the task section walks the deliverables one at a time in the source's order and numbering, working each hard one through: a rebuilt homework page folded them into checklists, taught the two hardest with one tiny example and "repeat the procedure", and renumbered and merged tasks, misleading a reader going between page and handout. Flag where the source itself is wrong, beside the step it affects: the original caught a handout's wrong table value and mux INIT; the rebuild dropped them. Ground the steps in the reader's own tools and starting point: the rebuild gave QuestaSim steps to a reader with a local GHDL harness, and took their finished files for their starting point.

## Words and trust

- **Words:** cut repetition that adds no understanding, not useful summaries, labels or worked examples. Skip kickers, subtitles restating headings, hints rewording labels, advice on reading a number, and page-making notes (where it saves, review rounds, who checked what, earlier drafts, source keys, tracker/date lines, reply-button instructions, "how this was made" sections): readers took them as filler. The headline number appears once on the first screen; keep cues that help the reader act. Write about the subject, not the conversation that produced the page: supplied figures are inputs, with a control when the result depends on them; earlier work appears as data, named with its sources.
- **Basis and honesty:** link claims to sources beside the evidence, especially actionable values, and give each candidate's datasheet or shop links one consistent place on it, not in prose. What a number rests on (method, assumptions, measured comparison, uncertainty) is content, not filler: keep it nearby, with detail in reference; a page cutting it with the filler lost. State an item's uncertainty once, as a status, not in every cell. Unknown is not no; a security claim stays exactly as strong as its source. Mark the page's own reasoning beside its claim; omit assumptions that would not change the reader's action.

## Showing

- **Show the subject:** where the subject has a structure or appearance, show it: a block diagram, the system in its context, the flow of the process being changed, or a photo, render or mockup when one exists. Charts of its numbers are not a picture of it: a plan with only Gantt and load charts never showed what it would build. Import real photos, drawings or datasheet crops of the thing being judged as local files, readable in size and captioned with what each proves; catalogue thumbnails that prove nothing do not count. Keep source links, and use a linked still for media that cannot work offline. No `file://` links in pages others receive; they point into one person's machine. Show a process whose order matters whole, beside the list of its steps, its labels a few words while the text carries the sentence. A circuit is a ```` ```schematic ```` netlist whose parts you place relative to each other, so the wiring you state is the wiring drawn (`kit schematic`): label parts by name, link them to the prose with `data-ref`, and draw only what the section explains, a stage or loop rather than the whole board. Hand-build inline SVG for a picture Mermaid and a schematic cannot draw, or parts that move or respond beyond highlighting and the states a step or choice sets by ref: a `viewBox` about 360 wide with 14-unit text reads on a phone, its width capped for wide screens; strokes and text in `currentColor` (`var(--ink)`), other colours in CSS from the kit variables, `var(--accent)` for the part in question; each part a `<g data-ref>` that its name in the prose shares. An SVG file in `<img>` follows the theme too, but its parts cannot be linked, and one a client component draws keeps the file's own colours.
- **Charts:** add zoom, legend toggles, animated switching or click-to-drill only when they help answer the question. Give exact values without hover, through a readout or disclosed table. A single value is a number (`.stats`), not a one-bar chart. Colour what the reader compares consistently across charts and views; greying every mark but one looked washed out.
- **Layout:** give a section the weight of its question rather than the shape of the one before; a page whose sections all share one shape reads as a template. Prose linked to a figure, diagram or code shares a screen with it in `.beside`, which keeps the figure in view while the text scrolls: readers of stacked pages zoomed out until nothing was readable. A box says its controls act on what is inside it, so add `.panel` only where a static figure must read as one object; pages where every block sat in the same card read as monotone. On phones, stack and reflow: nothing scrolls sideways but data tables, wide formulas and diagrams too wide to shrink to readable text.
- **Language:** write in the language the reader uses with you, asking when the source's could be meant, and keep the source's terms where the reader must find them in it; a rebuild for a reader chatting in English about a Slovenian handout chose without a rule. A page not in English sets `lang` in its frontmatter, and `labels` translated from `kit labels`; English controls on a Slovenian page read as unfinished.
