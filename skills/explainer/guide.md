# What made pages better or worse

Each point comes from comparing generated pages side by side. Use judgment where your subject differs.

## The reader

- **Decision first:** write it like a spec, not a landing page: the finding in the first sentence or figure, a figure made for it, a short interpretation. Start from the decision the reader faces and the one relation that settles it (for "how much", the step from one size to the next, not the totals), and cut what does not change that decision: decorative illustrations, circuits or protocol detail beside the point, and sections that do not bear on the choice. On a page for understanding or acting, cut by the reader's goal and fold useful depth the main path does not need. Ask for the decision after the evidence it rests on and on it: when the options are a comparison's rows, the rows are the `Question` (`rows`, `cols`), with `recommended` on yours, each cell a few words or a number so it fits a phone.
- **Context before findings:** judge what the reader knows of this project apart from their skill in the field: an expert new to it still needs what the thing is and what happened before a finding about it, in the first sentence when one does it, else in the sections before. Identify every name, part, revision, acronym and person in plain words where it first appears; put a section after the ones it depends on (the system before its failure, both options before their comparison). Then read it top to bottom as someone who has seen none of the sources.
- **Questions first:** organise around the reader's questions: pages following the data's hierarchy (phases, people, items) buried the first answer five clicks deep. Serving a decider and the people doing the work, start with the decider's questions and give the others their own sections. Aim for about one screen per reader question. Titles and headings plainly name what the page or section covers, in a few words ("Mixing", not "Mixing: why the image appears"), the key term optionally in `*em*`, which takes the accent; the answer, task or question goes in the section's first sentence or figure, and a second topic gets its own `###`. A page with two or more kinds of view has the rail; a page of a few sections needs none, and one readers cannot scan asks for it with `contents: true`. On a long page, group it: `##` chapters with `###` topics, which the rail indents under them; `---` marks a chapter break when space is not enough; a page several authors write can be split into `.mdx` parts, each `<Part />` on a line of its own.
- **Show the main path, fold the rest:** write as much as helps, but a section shows only its main path: the idea with its figure and, where they apply, the key formula set as a display equation, one example and the check; what the reader needs to act, answer or judge stays beside the task or question. Supporting derivations, further examples, edge cases, side notes (a notation that differs from the source's, another convention, how another chapter's case differs) and textbook references go in `<details>` folds, each summary the question it answers ("Why the factor 2?"), never "More", two or three a section; `View`, `Stepper` and a `Question`'s answer disclose too.
- **First screen:** the first sentence or figure gives the finding, and the first figure fits whole below the title. Subtitles, section intros, kickers, a "Decide:" line and meta lines are off by default: each adds a type level to say what the screen already shows. Three text roles (display, text, label) are enough, and all text inside a figure or control is label. When two numbers decide (cost against latency), the first sentence carries the one the reader acts on and the first figure shows both on its marks. On a how-to, the first task's section comes first, its first step and any warm-up beside it, with what it depends on; a choice is not such a task and follows its evidence. Say what was priced or measured: a plan opening on price without it failed when presented. Keep later controls with their questions: pages putting every filter, summary number and caveat up front read as a wall.

## Many records

- **Story in front, records behind:** the story is the subject drawn and explained; a status summary or a table of counts is not one. Keep many records (tasks, costs, findings, requirements) in one data collection and compute every number in the story from it. Story sections give the essence, each claim linking to the branch of the complete list it rests on (illustrative: "Spike boards: 12 tasks, 70 h →"). The list stays on the main page, every item in the source's own order (its numbering, sequence or chronology), grouped with subtotals; views add depth (a static `View` per record for a few, one filled from `records` for many or heavy ones). Sort by value only where the ranking is the answer, and call it a ranking. A view tells one thing's own story, not the page's table filtered to rows that are mostly zero.
- **Hierarchy as disclosure:** a collection goes in `Records`, grouped by its own fields, each row a summary line with its numbers. A filter highlights matches in the tree rather than flattening it. Each item that opens says what it is, why it matters and, where the source says, what done means; each group gets a sentence on what it delivers. A row carries only what readers compare across rows; the rest goes, unrepeated, into the opened item. A marker (a `.chip` beside its word) encodes a category readers scan across rows, never decoration.

## Judging every item

- **Each item whole, where it is judged:** when the reader must approve, decide or answer for every item (a decisions review, an approval list), show each one complete with its question beside it: the situation (what the reader does and what happens), what happens now, the alternative (and what comparable tools do, when known) and a recommendation. A fifty-decision review built as collapsed groups with a drill-down per item forced its owner into every item for context. For single-choice reviews, `Records` with `open`, `reply` and `width: "full"` lets items be read and answered in their rows; an item with its own options or recommendation takes a `Question`, kept with the item, not behind a click or collapsed group.
- **Only what needs the reader:** list items needing a call; fold the rest. The reply bar shows answered/total; keep any count in the text consistent with it. Label options A/B/C beside their evidence, and when a heading already asks the question, leave out `legend` rather than say it twice. Mark the recommendation with `recommended` rather than in the label, and give a `note` where the reader may want to say why. Include "both" or "test first" when they are real outcomes, and "need more context" so a gap in the page becomes an answer, not a guess (when the reader must pick one, a `note` takes the gap instead). Forced single choices can misrepresent decisions: use `Question multiple` when several may be chosen (`Records.reply` is single-choice). Saved answers live in browser storage, which may not last for a `file://` page; reply's "Copy reply", or Send on a page `serve` serves, keeps important ones outside it.
- **Ask when the source cannot say:** when the source gives an item less than the reader needs to judge it, ask whoever asked for the page before building. A value the source does not give stays empty, never inferred from its group's heading.

## Explaining

- **Interaction that reveals:** a control answers a question the reader has, or captures a decision, and changes something visible at once; one per figure is usually enough. Readers valued hiding a dominant part to see the rest, presets that honestly end in "nothing fits", and clicking a mark or row to open its view. Start a model at its smallest instructive size. Label what a control changes, and offer a way back to a meaningful default. Compare states with aligned small multiples, not a switched-away view. Sentences, counts, labels and explanations that depend on a control follow it or name the default state. What no control changes (table rows, item lists, a view's body) stays outside client components, so it shows before scripts run, prints, and keeps the layout still. Ask reader-only decisions on the page as reply questions.
- **Markdown first:** prose, lists, tables and formulas stay Markdown, also between a client component's tags (its `{children}`). Inside a JSX expression such as `items.map(...)`, backticks are plain text and a `{x}` between them is evaluated. For a computed link destination, use `<a href={...}>`, not braces in a Markdown destination. `kit types > tsconfig.json` prepares the config; `bun --check` checks the imported TS/TSX and reports success or errors (not MDX expressions).
- **Stepping:** step through something only when its order is the point, and offer several playable cases, each step showing where we are, what just changed and what has accumulated; one narrow walkthrough lost to broader pages every time. Structure that does not change, such as a total built from parts or a system and its connections, is one labelled drawing with values on the marks (a flowchart whose stages carry their size or time in the label or on the edge; when the text and a table refer to its parts, number them with tags, `kit mermaid`): Back, Play and Next around it cost a row and reveal nothing, and an animated sequence diagram nobody needed is the same mistake.
- **Study material:** start from the source's figures; a page that added every widget to a course's slides lost the point. Worked examples come before practice: "try first" suits revision, not missing basics. Keep a formula sheet as reference (`kit math.css`). Practice uses plausible choices (`Question` with `answer`) or a written answer (`Question` without options, the model answer inside), hiding the explanation and figures that give it away, and nothing above it (the worked example's values, a summary table) already states its result; its heading asks the question.
- **Reminders:** a term, identity or result from earlier material (another chapter, a prior course, a tool the reader half-knows) that a sentence uses goes in a `Recall` opened at each `Term` mention (a whole concept in a `View` the term leads to), not re-explained inline or in an aside everyone reads past.
- **Tasks and pitfalls:** set a task the reader solves in `<aside class="callout">` with its name in a `<b>` first ("Task 3"), a common mistake in a `class="caveat"` beside the step it affects, and steps done in order as a numbered list in `<div class="procedure">` (`kit theme`); a bold lead-in mid-paragraph hides all three.
- **Homework:** the task section walks the deliverables in the source's order and numbering, each hard one worked at its own scale, or as a rule with a way to check the result, with a code skeleton where the deliverable is code. What a step needs stays in the step, not in a fold. Flag where the source is wrong beside the step it affects, recomputed and quoting what the source prints: a rebuild reported a table error that did not exist and missed a real one. Ground the steps in the reader's own tools and starting point, and ask which toolchain they have; when a tool was not supplied, ask for it or have the reader run it bare for its usage.

## Words and trust

- **Words:** one idea per paragraph in short sentences: a second point, an aside or a warning starts its own paragraph or block. In running text, bold only a term where it is defined, never for stress or as a lead-in ("Example:"; a callout's `<b>` names its kind instead); citations and equation numbers at a paragraph's end rather than mid-sentence (background references in a fold), and a few kinds of block per section, used consistently. Cut repetition that adds no understanding, not useful summaries, labels or worked examples. Skip captions restating headings, hints rewording labels, advice on reading a number, and page-making notes (where it saves, review rounds, who checked what, earlier drafts, source keys, tracker/date lines, reply-button instructions, "how this was made" sections): readers took them as filler. No summary readout repeats the headline number (labels on its own marks are not repeats); keep cues that help the reader act. Schedules and owners belong to the tracker; a decision appears as what was decided and why. Write about the subject, not the conversation that produced the page: supplied figures are inputs, with a control when the result depends on them; earlier work appears as data, its sources linked beside it.
- **Basis and honesty:** put sources and qualifications that affect how a claim reads beside it, especially for actionable values, and each candidate's datasheet or shop links in one consistent place on it, not in prose. What a number rests on is content, not filler: shared method and assumptions go once in a closing basis paragraph, detail in reference. State an item's uncertainty once, as a status, not in every cell. Unknown is not no; a security claim stays exactly as strong as its source, and the page's own reasoning is marked beside its claim. Omit assumptions that would not change the reader's action.

## Showing

- **A figure carries each answer:** by default, and the context before it too (what diverged from what, what happened when). More than about 150 words in a section with no figure, table, diagram or code read as a wall, and `check` warns about one. Draw a process whole, its labels a few words; cut a figure that decorates, never the one that shows the answer.
- **Show the subject:** by default, and always when explaining a system or process: a block diagram, the system in its context, the flow of the process being changed, or a photo, render or mockup when one exists. Charts of its numbers are not a picture of it. Import real photos, drawings or datasheet crops of the thing being judged, readable in size and captioned with what each proves; catalogue thumbnails that prove nothing do not count. Link a still for media that cannot work offline; no `file://` links, which point into one person's machine. A circuit is a ```` ```schematic ```` netlist, so the wiring you state is the wiring drawn (`kit schematic`): label parts by name, link them to the prose with `data-ref`, and draw only what the section explains, a stage or loop rather than the whole board. Hand-build inline SVG for a picture Mermaid and a schematic cannot draw, or parts that move or respond beyond highlighting and the states a step or choice sets by ref: a `viewBox` about 360 wide with 14-unit text reads on a phone; strokes and text in `currentColor`, other colours from the kit variables, `var(--accent)` for the part in question; each part a `<g data-ref>` that its name in the prose shares. An SVG file in `<img>` follows the theme but cannot link its parts. Ink-on-white scans take `class="ink"` to blend into the background or `class="paper"` to stay a page; neither for photos.
- **A whole system is a tour:** open with one map of the whole: a node, with its size or role, for every part the tour opens, what flows between them on the edges, and beside it the list linking each part to its view. Each area is then a section or `View`, split as finely as helps; it opens with its own diagram or chart (its parts, data model, states or numbers), and each section in it carries one too, never text alone. Draw examples as walkthroughs: one real request or event followed through the parts, as a sequence diagram or a `Stepper` naming them in turn. A register of every file, module or setting is reference behind the tour, linked from its parts, never its spine: a 1,800-part tree with all-prose views went unread.
- **Charts:** choose the form from the claim: sections making different claims should not share a shape (bars on one scale for a gap, an area for energy moved, a `Chart` line over stacked columns for a surplus that disappears). Put names and values on the marks, and keep a legend, axis title or gridline only when it tells the reader what the marks do not. Use one accent with one meaning across the page, the finding, on the same thing in every figure (never the problem in one and the fix in the next): series default to muted inks told apart by lightness, a line also by its dash, so put the accent on the series or bar the page is about, and mark other distinctions by the accent's hollow or hatch state, each with one meaning, named once on a mark; keep each compared thing in the same slot across charts and views. When names crowd, shorten them, drop a series or give the figure `height` before turning on a legend. Keep raw values for the arithmetic and show one precision, the one the decision needs (`format: { digits: 0 }`), in text, marks and table alike. Say what a region or point of a figure means on it, in words (a `Plot` note: "surplus goes to the grid", or an arrow for a change the reader should follow), rather than in a legend or caption. Give exact values without hover, on the marks, through a readout or disclosed table; when the reader picks one of a plot's columns (a month of twelve), make the choice the axis (`axis: true` in `Explore`), so the picked column's key gives its values where a row of stats would repeat them beside the figure. A single value is a number (`.stats`), not a one-bar chart. Zoom, legend toggles and click-to-drill only when they help answer the question. Motion only where it shows the phenomenon: a figure changes from its current state after interaction, and nothing animates or replays on arrival.
- **Colour:** the accent is yours to choose, from the subject or the user's or project's brand, in one `:root` rule of `light-dark()` pairs: `--accent: light-dark(#1f5fbf, #7fb0ff)`, dark enough for text on the light paper and light enough on the dark, with `--accent-ink` beside it when white text does not read on the light one. Where the categories are colours by nature (energy sources, teams, parties, lines on a map), give them real hues the same way in `--c1`..`--c6`; other series keep the muted inks. `check` warns about a token too faint for its use.
- **Layout:** give a section the weight of its question rather than the shape of the one before; sections sharing one shape read as a template. Prose linked to a figure, diagram or code shares a screen with it in `.beside`, which keeps the figure in view while the text scrolls: readers of stacked pages zoomed out until nothing was readable. Put a list and its drawing in `<div class="beside" data-width="full">`, list first, with each item carrying the space-separated `data-ref` names of its parts. With a heading and caption around the drawing, the list starts at the prose column and the pinned figure takes only the width its drawing can use. Put an `<img>` directly in the figure rather than in a Markdown image paragraph. If a diagram still scrolls sideways, try a taller layout. Keep a wide comparison full width above the list. Figures sit plain on the page, their controls right above what they act on. On phones, stack and reflow: nothing scrolls sideways but data tables, wide formulas and diagrams too wide to shrink to readable text.
- **Language:** write in the language the reader uses with you, asking when the source's could be meant, and keep the source's terms where the reader must find them in it. A page not in English sets `lang` in its frontmatter, and `labels` translated from `kit labels`; English controls on a Slovenian page read as unfinished.
