# What made pages better or worse

Each point comes from comparing generated pages side by side. Use judgment where your subject differs.

- **Questions first:** list the questions the reader brings (who does what, what costs most, where the risk is) and give each a section whose heading answers it. Pages that followed the data's own hierarchy (phases, then people, then items) made readers click five levels deep for the first answer. When one page serves both the person who decides and the people doing the work, start from the decider's questions and give the others their own sections.
- **First screen:** the answer in one sentence and the figure that shows it. Pages that put every filter, summary number, and caveat up front read as a wall; controls for later questions belong in those sections.
- **Interaction that reveals:** each control answers a question the reader would ask and changes something visible at once. The ones readers valued: hiding the dominant part so the rest becomes readable, playing through several scenarios rather than one path, presets that can honestly end in "nothing fits", clicking a mark or row to open its own view.
- **Answers visible:** a section shows its answer in the section itself; folds and views hold the detail a reader checks, not the finding. A people-by-phase chart on the overview answers "who does what"; six person views opened one at a time do not. A view tells one thing's own story rather than the page's table filtered to rows that are mostly zero.
- **Order:** list items in the source's own order: its numbering, sequence, or chronology. Sort by value only where the ranking is the answer, and call it a ranking. A plan whose tasks were sorted by hours lost the lead, who knew them by their work-list numbers.
- **Explanations:** offer several playable cases. Each step shows where we are, what just changed, and what has accumulated so far. One narrow walkthrough lost to broader pages every time.
- **Stepping:** step through something when its order is the point: an algorithm's rounds, a sequence of messages, a derivation. A total built from parts, such as a cost waterfall or a breakdown, reads at a glance: show it finished with the values on the marks. Back, Play and Next around it cost a row and reveal nothing. Steppers work on HTML and hand-built SVG; a Mermaid diagram shows whole.
- **Study material:** a page that took a course's slides and added every widget it could lost the point. Start from the questions the reader must answer and the source's own figures; give each idea at most one interaction, where moving it builds intuition; put answers behind a click so the reader tries first; end with a formula sheet. Include `kit:reply.js` so the reader's inputs and notes survive a reload; give inputs ids. Fidelity to the source beats extra scope.
- **Words:** each line says something the page does not already say. Skip the kicker above the title or a section heading, subtitles that restate a heading, hints that reword their label, advice on how to read a number, and notes about the page itself, such as where it saves or how it was made (review rounds, who checked what, earlier drafts); the headline number appears once on the first screen. A short cue for an interaction the reader could not guess, such as clicking a bar to drill down, earns its place. Readers took the rest as filler. Write about the subject, not the conversation that produced the page. A figure the person who asked mentioned in passing is an input: use it without quoting it back, and make it a control when the result depends on it. Earlier work appears only as data the page uses, such as measured hours behind an estimate, named once with the sources. What a number rests on is content, not filler: the method, the values assumed, the measured work it was compared with, and how sure it is. Keep it beside the number; a page whose reader must defend its numbers lost when it cut that basis along with the filler.
- **Live text:** a headline or count that depends on controls updates with them, or is phrased as a claim about the default view. Labels and explanations never contradict the current state.
- **Surfaces:** a box tells the reader that its controls act on what is inside it. The kit boxes a figure that holds inputs, tabs or a stepper and leaves a static chart, diagram or table plain on the page, in line with the text; add `.panel` only when a static figure must read as one object. Pages where every block sat in the same card read as monotone.
- **Rhythm:** give a section the weight of its question rather than the shape of the one before. Set supporting points beside their figure (`.beside`), sources and secondary method notes as `.minor` (a method the reader must defend is a section of its own), and the one place where the reader decides or plays as a `.band`. A page whose sections all share one shape reads as a template.
- **Charts:** use the zoom, legend toggles, animated switching between views, and click-to-drill that ECharts already has. Give exact values without hover, through a readout or a table behind a disclosure. A single value is a number (`.stats`), not a one-bar chart.
- **Diagrams:** Mermaid for structure, flows, sequences, and states; top-down layouts stay readable on phones. For a process whose order matters, show the diagram whole beside its steps as a list whose key terms light their part (`data-ref`). Keep node labels to a few words and let the text carry the sentence; longer labels wrap at about 30 characters into tall boxes. Hand-build SVG when a diagram must step or respond in ways highlighting cannot. An SVG file in `<img>` follows the theme through `currentColor` and the kit variables (`var(--ink)`, `var(--accent)`); write the SVG inline when its parts must be linked or interactive.
- **Navigation:** link every view from the overview with a table or list whose link text names the view briefly ("Blaž", "1.6.3 Login form"), and keep the `.back` link in each view head. On a page for planning, deciding or reviewing a set of items (tasks, costs, findings, requirements), that list is complete: every item on the main page, grouped with subtotals, each row opening its view. Views add depth; without the full list, the reader hopped between views to see the plan. `kit:views.js` builds breadcrumbs, previous and next, and on pages with views inside views a contents rail from those links; a section with several folds gets Expand all.
- **Asking the reader:** when the page ends in a choice only the reader can make, ask it on the page as a reply question rather than in chat text.
- **Phones:** stack and reflow. Nothing scrolls sideways except data tables.
- **Language:** a page not in English sets `<html lang>` and, in any of its scripts, `var kitLabels = { ... }` with the kit's own buttons, accessible names and messages in its language (`kit <file>` lists the keys); English controls on a Slovenian page read as unfinished.
- **Honesty:** unknown is not no; a claim never exceeds its source, especially about security. Where the page's own reasoning goes past its sources and the reader would act on it, say so once, beside that claim; leave out assumptions that would not change what the reader does.
- **Colour:** colour what the reader compares, and give each compared thing (a phase, an option, a series) the same colour on every chart and view of the page. Grey is for context the section is not about, such as the rest of a total or a baseline; the accent marks the headline and the mark a readout points at. Pages that greyed every mark but one read as washed out. Use the theme tokens so light and dark both work.
