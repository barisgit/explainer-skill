# What made pages better or worse

Each point comes from comparing generated pages side by side. Use judgment where your subject differs.

## The reader

- **Questions first:** list the questions the reader brings and give each a section whose heading states the answer and whose body shows it; folds and views hold the detail a reader checks, not the finding. Pages that followed the data's own hierarchy (phases, then people, then items) made readers click five levels deep for the first answer. When one page serves both a decider and the people doing the work, start from the decider's questions and give the others their own sections. Answer headings are too long to scan in the contents rail, where readers lost track of what the page holds: give each section a `data-nav` label of one to three words.
- **First screen:** the answer as the headline, in one sentence (study material: its questions), and the figure that shows it. For a reader not already inside the subject (a briefing, someone new, a presented page), one or two lines beneath the headline orient them: what this is, where it stands, what is wanted, and what is in and out of scope. A plan that opened on its price, with nothing on what was priced, failed when it was presented. Pages that put every filter, summary number and caveat up front read as a wall; controls for later questions belong in those sections.

## Many records

- **Story in front, records behind:** keep many records (tasks, costs, findings, requirements) in one data collection and compute every number in the story from it. Story sections give the essence, each claim a link that opens the branch of the complete list it rests on (illustrative: "Spike boards: 12 tasks, 70 h →"). The list stays on the main page, every item in the source's own order (its numbering, sequence or chronology), grouped with subtotals; views add depth. Without it readers hopped between views to see the plan, and a plan sorted by hours lost the lead, who knew its tasks by their work-list numbers. Sort by value only where the ranking is the answer, and call it a ranking. A view tells one thing's own story, not the page's table filtered to rows that are mostly zero. A few records get one static `View` each; a dozen or more, or heavy ones, fill one `<View records={items} render={Item}>` from code, which prints only while open.
- **Hierarchy as disclosure:** nested records go in the kit's `Tree`, each row a summary line with its numbers. A filter highlights matches in the tree rather than flattening it into a filtered table. Each item that opens says what it is, why it matters, and what done means where the source says so; each group gets a sentence on what it delivers. A row carries only what readers compare across rows; the rest goes into the opened item, without repeating the row. A marker (a `.chip`, keyed in a `.legend`) encodes a category readers scan across rows, never decoration. A ninety-item plan shown as a flat table of hours and prices, never saying what each item was, left its lead unable to defend the price.

## Explaining

- **Interaction that reveals:** each control answers a question the reader would ask and changes something visible at once. The ones readers valued: hiding the dominant part so the rest becomes readable, playing through several scenarios rather than one path, presets that can honestly end in "nothing fits", clicking a mark or row to open its own view. A headline or count that depends on controls updates with them, or is phrased as a claim about the default view; labels and explanations never contradict the current state. What no control changes (table rows, item lists, a view's body) stays outside client components, so it shows before scripts run, prints, and keeps the layout still. When the page ends in a choice only the reader can make, ask it on the page as a reply question, not in chat.
- **Stepping:** step through something only when its order is the point. Offer several playable cases, each step showing where we are, what just changed and what has accumulated; one narrow walkthrough lost to broader pages every time. A total built from parts, such as a cost waterfall, reads at a glance: show it finished with the values on the marks, since Back, Play and Next around it cost a row and reveal nothing.
- **Study material:** a page that took a course's slides and added every widget it could lost the point. Start from the source's own figures, give each idea at most one interaction, where moving it builds intuition, and end with a formula sheet: a table with one row per formula, its name and its symbols (`kit math.css`). The reader tries first: a bounded answer gets a few plausible choices in a `Question` with `answer`, which holds the explanation and any figure that gives it away; choosing marks it and opens the explanation, kept across reloads, with no Reply button. One without options takes a written answer, its model answer folded inside. Headings here ask the question; one that states the answer gives it away.

## Words and trust

- **Words:** each line says something the page does not already say. Skip the kicker above a title or heading, subtitles that restate a heading, hints that reword their label, advice on how to read a number, and notes about the page itself (where it saves, review rounds, who checked what, earlier drafts); the headline number appears once on the first screen. A short cue for an interaction the reader could not guess earns its place. Readers took the rest as filler. Write about the subject, not the conversation that produced the page: a figure the person who asked mentioned in passing is an input, used without quoting it back and made a control when the result depends on it; earlier work appears only as data the page uses, such as measured hours behind an estimate, named once with the sources.
- **Basis and honesty:** what a number rests on is content, not filler: the method, the values assumed, the measured work it was compared with, and how sure it is. Keep it beside the number, and give a method the reader must defend a section of its own; a page whose reader must defend its numbers lost when it cut that basis along with the filler. Unknown is not no, and a claim about security stays exactly as strong as its source. Mark the page's own reasoning once, beside its claim; leave out assumptions that would not change what the reader does.

## Showing

- **Show the subject:** where the subject has a structure or appearance, show it: a block diagram, the system in its context, the flow of the process being changed, or a photo, render or mockup when one exists. Charts of its numbers are not a picture of it: a plan with only a Gantt chart and load charts never showed what it would build. Show a process whose order matters whole, beside the list of its steps, its labels a few words while the text carries the sentence. Hand-build inline SVG for a picture Mermaid cannot draw, or for parts that step or respond beyond highlighting: a `viewBox` about 360 wide with 14-unit text reads on a phone, its displayed width capped for wide screens; strokes and text in `currentColor` (`var(--ink)`), other colours in CSS from the kit variables, `var(--accent)` for the part in question; each part a `<g data-ref>` that its name in the prose shares. An SVG file in `<img>` follows the theme the same way, but its parts cannot be linked.
- **Charts:** use the zoom, legend toggles, animated switching between views, and click-to-drill that ECharts already has. Give exact values without hover, through a readout or a table behind a disclosure. A single value is a number (`.stats`), not a one-bar chart. Pages that greyed every mark but one read as washed out: colour what the reader compares, the same way on every chart and view.
- **Layout:** give a section the weight of its question rather than the shape of the one before; a page whose sections all share one shape reads as a template. A box tells the reader that its controls act on what is inside it, so add `.panel` only where a static figure must read as one object; pages where every block sat in the same card read as monotone. On phones, stack and reflow: nothing scrolls sideways except data tables and wide formulas.
- **Language:** a page not in English sets `lang` in its frontmatter. The kit's buttons and messages are English: run `kit labels`, translate the values and paste the block into the frontmatter. English controls on a Slovenian page read as unfinished.

## Writing the page

- **One tag per interaction:** a slider, switch or option selector over a figure is an `Explore`, a step-through a `Stepper` (`restartOn` starts it over when the case changes), a drill-down a `View`, a table from data a `Table`, a chart a `Chart` or `Plot` with JSON formats, a self-check or written answer a `Question`. A `"use client"` component is for behaviour none of them covers.
- **Markdown first:** prose, lists, tables and TeX formulas (`$...$` in a sentence, `$$` on lines of their own, `\$` for a dollar) stay Markdown, also between a client component's tags, which it places with `{children}`; a `##` first there opens the section. JSX returned from an expression such as `items.map(...)` is not Markdown: backticks there are plain text and a `{x}` between them is evaluated. In your own component, controls with ids are kept across reloads: set their state in `onInput` or `onChange` with a functional `setState((s) => ...)`, since merging into a closure's old state loses restored values. Typecheck: `kit types > tsconfig.json`, then `bun check`.
