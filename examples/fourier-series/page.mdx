## Six questions to answer before you read on

Write what you think in the box, then open the answer.

<div class="questions">

<Question name="a1" legend="What does a Fourier series say, and why can sines and cosines, which are smooth, build a square wave with sharp corners?">

A periodic signal can be written as a constant plus sines and cosines whose frequencies are whole multiples of the signal's own frequency. Each multiple, a harmonic, has one amplitude for its cosine and one for its sine.

No finite sum of smooth waves has a corner, but each extra harmonic fixes more of the error, and the sum gets as close as you like to the square wave in the mean-square sense. The first section below shows why the amounts can be read off one at a time.

</Question>

<Question name="a2" legend="For a square wave that is −1 on the left half-period and +1 on the right half, which harmonics appear, and how fast do their amplitudes shrink?">

Only sines with odd $n$: $b_n = 4/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$, and zero for even $n$ and for every cosine. The amplitudes fall like $1/n$, so $4/\pi$, $4/(3\pi)$, $4/(5\pi)$, …

</Question>

<Question name="a3" legend={<>The sawtooth <i>f</i>(<i>x</i>) = <i>x</i> on (−π, π) also falls like 1/n. What is different about its coefficients compared with the square wave?</>}>

$b_n = 2(-1)^{n+1}/n$. Every harmonic appears, not just the odd ones, and the signs alternate: +2, −1, +2/3, −1/2, … Both waves are odd, so both have only sines.

</Question>

<Question name="a4" legend="You go from 9 to 99 harmonics of the square wave. Does the overshoot next to a jump get lower, stay the same, or get higher? Where does the bump move?">

It stays about the same height: roughly 9% of the jump, a peak near 1.18 for a wave that jumps from −1 to +1. The bump gets narrower and moves closer to the jump, at $x = \pi/(2k)$ for $k$ terms. The section on the bump lets you check.

</Question>

<Question name="a5" legend="If the bump never shrinks, in what sense does the series converge to the square wave? What value does it give exactly at the jump?">

At every point away from a jump the sum converges to the signal, because the bump has moved past any fixed point. The mean-square error also goes to zero, since the bump gets narrower while its height stays put. What does not happen is uniform convergence. Just beside a jump the sum is still near 0 while the signal is near ±1, so the largest error stays large, close to half the jump. The overshoot above the plateau tends to about 9% of the jump rather than to zero. At the jump itself, the series gives the midpoint of the two sides, 0 for the square wave.

</Question>

<Question name="a6" legend={<>Without integrating: which coefficients are zero for <i>x</i>², for <i>x</i>³, and for <i>x</i>² + <i>x</i> on (−π, π)?</>}>

$x^2$ is even: all $b_n$ are zero, only cosines (and $a_0$) remain. $x^3$ is odd: $a_0$ and all $a_n$ are zero, only sines remain. $x^2 + x$ is the sum of an even and an odd part, so it has both and no term is guaranteed to vanish. Its cosines come from $x^2$ and its sines from $x$.

</Question>

</div>

## Why sines and cosines are enough

Take a signal that repeats every 2π. Its Fourier series is a constant plus sines and cosines of whole-number multiples of the base frequency:

$$
f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx\right)
$$

Two facts make this work. First, every term repeats after 2π, so any sum of them does too. Second, the terms are **orthogonal**: over one period, the integral of the product of two different terms is zero, and for positive whole numbers the product of a sine or cosine with itself integrates to π. The constant term is its own case: its square integrates to 2π. Multiply the signal by one term and integrate, and every other term drops out. That leaves one number, the amount of that term in the signal. It is the formula for the coefficients used below.

The figure above is the series in action. With one term the square wave is a single sine of amplitude 4/π. Each added sine puts back detail the previous sum lacked, and the corners get steeper.

<li>Cosine coefficients first. The square wave is odd and $\cos nx$ is even, so the integrand is odd and its integral over a symmetric interval is zero. The same argument gives $a_0 = 0$.</li>

<li>$f$ and $\sin nx$ are both odd, so their product is even: integrate over half the period and double. On $(0, \pi)$ the square wave is 1.</li>
<li>Integrate $\sin nx$ and put in the limits. $\cos 0 = 1$ supplies the 1.</li>
<li>$\cos n\pi$ is $-1$ or $+1$ as $n$ is odd or even. Even harmonics cancel; odd ones keep $4/(n\pi)$.</li>
<li>Put the coefficients back into the series. This is the sum being built at the top of the page.</li>

b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \sin nx \, dx

$$

<section>
<Symmetry>
An even function ($f(-x) = f(x)$) is a mirror image across the vertical axis; an odd one flips sign. Cosines are even and sines are odd, so a mirror-symmetric signal can only contain cosines and an odd one only sines. Try the three cases; the coefficients are computed by numerical integration.
</Symmetry>

Spotting the symmetry first halves the work, and saves a mistake: a stray cosine term in an odd function's answer is wrong. The half ramp is the sum of its even part, a triangle, and its odd part, a straight line, so it has both kinds of term.

</section>

## Everything above on one table

| Rule | Formula | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Series, period $2\pi$ | $\displaystyle f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx\right)$ | |
| Cosine coefficients | $\displaystyle a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cos nx \, dx$ | $n \ge 0$ |
| Sine coefficients | $\displaystyle b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \sin nx \, dx$ | $n \ge 1$ |
| Series, period $T$ | $\displaystyle f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n \cos n\omega t + b_n \sin n\omega t\right)$ | $\omega = 2\pi/T$ |
| Coefficients, period $T$ | $\displaystyle a_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(t) \cos n\omega t \, dt$ | |
| | $\displaystyle b_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(t) \sin n\omega t \, dt$ | |
| Orthogonality | $\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin mx \sin nx \, dx = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos mx \cos nx \, dx = 0$ | $m \ne n$; both are $\pi$ when $m = n \ge 1$ |
| | $\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin mx \cos nx \, dx = 0$ | all $m$, $n$ |
| | $\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos nx \, dx = 0, \quad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1 \, dx = 2\pi$ | $n \ge 1$ |
| Even $f$ | $\displaystyle b_n = 0, \quad a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} f(x) \cos nx \, dx$ | $f(-x) = f(x)$ |
| Odd $f$ | $\displaystyle a_0 = a_n = 0, \quad b_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} f(x) \sin nx \, dx$ | $f(-x) = -f(x)$ |
| Square wave | $\displaystyle b_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$ | odd $n$; 0 for even $n$; the wave is $\pm 1$ and odd |
| | $\displaystyle f(x) = \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{n \text{ odd}} \frac{\sin nx}{n}$ | |
| Sawtooth | $\displaystyle b_n = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$ | $f(x) = x$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$ |
| | $\displaystyle f(x) = 2 \left( \sin x - \frac{\sin 2x}{2} + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} - \dots \right)$ | |
| At a jump | $\displaystyle \frac{f(x^+) + f(x^-)}{2}$ | the midpoint of the two one-sided limits |
| Gibbs overshoot | $\displaystyle \max \to \frac{2}{\pi} \operatorname{Si}(\pi) \approx 1.179$ | square wave $\pm 1$: the $k$-term sum peaks at $x = \pi/(2k)$, an overshoot of $0.0895 \times$ the jump |
| | $\displaystyle \operatorname{Si}(\pi) = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin t}{t} \, dt \approx 1.852$ | |
| Parseval | $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)^2 \, dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n^2 + b_n^2\right)$ | after $N$ terms the mean-square error is half the sum of the squared omitted coefficients |

<p class="caveat"><b>Notation</b>Different books split the constant term differently. Here it is $a_0/2$, which makes the same $a_n$ formula work for $n = 0$. Books that write plain $a_0$ use the average, $a_0 = (1/2\pi) \int f \, dx$.</p>
