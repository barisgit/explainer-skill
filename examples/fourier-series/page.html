A square wave is a sum of sines, and the bump at its jump never goes away

Sines at 1×, 3×, 5× the base frequency, with relative amplitudes 1, ⅓, ⅕, … (scaled by 4/π for a wave of height ±1), add up to a square wave. Drag the slider to add terms, switch to the sawtooth, and tick the box to see each sine.

Six questions to answer before you read on

Write what you think in the box, then open the answer.

  1. What does a Fourier series say, and why can sines and cosines, which are smooth, build a square wave with sharp corners?

    Show answer

    A periodic signal can be written as a constant plus sines and cosines whose frequencies are whole multiples of the signal's own frequency. Each multiple, a harmonic, has one amplitude for its cosine and one for its sine.

    No finite sum of smooth waves has a corner, but each extra harmonic fixes more of the error, and the sum gets as close as you like to the square wave in the mean-square sense. The first section below shows why the amounts can be read off one at a time.

  2. For a square wave that is −1 on the left half-period and +1 on the right half, which harmonics appear, and how fast do their amplitudes shrink?

    Show answer

    Only sines with odd n: bn = 4/(nπ) for odd n, and zero for even n and for every cosine. The amplitudes fall like 1/n, so 4/π, 4/(3π), 4/(5π), …

  3. The sawtooth f(x) = x on (−π, π) also falls like 1/n. What is different about its coefficients compared with the square wave?

    Show answer

    bn = 2(−1)n+1/n. Every harmonic appears, not just the odd ones, and the signs alternate: +2, −1, +2/3, −1/2, … Both waves are odd, so both have only sines.

  4. You go from 9 to 99 harmonics of the square wave. Does the overshoot next to a jump get lower, stay the same, or get higher? Where does the bump move?

    Show answer

    It stays about the same height: roughly 9% of the jump, a peak near 1.18 for a wave that jumps from −1 to +1. The bump gets narrower and moves closer to the jump, at x = π/(2k) for k terms. The section on the bump lets you check.

  5. If the bump never shrinks, in what sense does the series converge to the square wave? What value does it give exactly at the jump?

    Show answer

    At every point away from a jump the sum converges to the signal, because the bump has moved past any fixed point. The mean-square error also goes to zero, since the bump gets narrower while its height stays put. What does not happen is uniform convergence. Just beside a jump the sum is still near 0 while the signal is near ±1, so the largest error stays large, close to half the jump. The overshoot above the plateau tends to about 9% of the jump rather than to zero. At the jump itself, the series gives the midpoint of the two sides, 0 for the square wave.

  6. Without integrating: which coefficients are zero for x², for x³, and for x² + x on (−π, π)?

    Show answer

    x² is even: all bn are zero, only cosines (and a0) remain. x³ is odd: a0 and all an are zero, only sines remain. x² + x is the sum of an even and an odd part, so it has both and no term is guaranteed to vanish. Its cosines come from x² and its sines from x.

Why sines and cosines are enough

Take a signal that repeats every 2π. Its Fourier series is a constant plus sines and cosines of whole-number multiples of the base frequency:

Two facts make this work. First, every term repeats after 2π, so any sum of them does too. Second, the terms are orthogonal: over one period, the integral of the product of two different terms is zero, and for positive whole numbers the product of a sine or cosine with itself integrates to π. The constant term is its own case: its square integrates to 2π. Multiply the signal by one term and integrate, and every other term drops out. That leaves one number, the amount of that term in the signal. It is the formula for the coefficients used below.

The figure above is the series in action. With one term the square wave is a single sine of amplitude 4/π. Each added sine puts back detail the previous sum lacked, and the corners get steeper.

Pick a wave and step through its integral, one line at a time. Both waves are odd, so the cosine integrals vanish at once and only the sine integral takes work.

Here is the square wave from the top, close to its jump at x = 0. Drag the slider from 1 term up to 200 and watch two things: the width of the bump and its height.

The bump keeps its height and gets thinner. The typical error falls because the bump takes up less and less of the period, so the area under it goes to zero. The largest error does not fall: the bump stays, and just beside the jump the sum is still far from the wave. That is why the series converges at every fixed point away from the jump (convergence is not uniform) while no finite number of terms removes the bump.

An even function (f(−x) = f(x)) is a mirror image across the vertical axis; an odd one flips sign. Cosines are even and sines are odd, so a mirror-symmetric signal can only contain cosines and an odd one only sines. Try the three cases; the coefficients are computed by numerical integration.

Spotting the symmetry first halves the work, and saves a mistake: a stray cosine term in an odd function's answer is wrong. The half ramp is the sum of its even part, a triangle, and its odd part, a straight line, so it has both kinds of term.
